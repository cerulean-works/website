In 2018, if you asked engineers what the world least needs more of, they’d probably tell you it’s a new JavaScript framework. Now, it’s another AI lab.

Yet Cerulean is not a conventional laboratory. We do not intend to become one. We see our mission as demonstrating a new way of doing frontier AI research, one that gives society a shot at democratizing AI development and ensuring it’s done for the greater good.

We do not expect our research to generate profit, nor are we from a country leading the AI race. Nonetheless, we aim to prove that AI research labs can thrive at the crossroads of sufficient public sector support and dense, dedicated talent.

We believe that only through relentless openness can we, as humanity, navigate the complex socioeconomic landscape of AI development, ensuring this technology can be used for human good.

## Beyond winner-takes-all

Throughout history, it seems that the fewer stakeholders involved in the development of technology, the more likely it ultimately gets treated as a machine to extract profit rather than to benefit the world. No matter how well-intentioned a company’s founders are, over time, investors and the market push tech companies to forget their mandate to “don’t be evil” and instead embrace increasingly anti-consumer practices to pad their bottom lines.

We don’t treat this as pure greed, but as an inevitability of incentives. Rather than blaming companies for following the law and maximizing shareholder value, it's more effective to align incentives so that maximizing shareholder value also maximizes humanity’s benefit. This incentive wrangling may sound impossible, but an interesting case argues otherwise: democracy. Instead of trusting that nation leaders will always be benevolent, a democratic system shards incentives so broadly that it’s considerably harder to disavow a large group of people to please another. Similarly, by significantly expanding who gets a say in how AI is developed, we see promise that players in this field would be incentivized to entertain everyone’s incentives during this process. We see AI as impactful as, if not more than, the Industrial Revolution, and believe we must treat it with similar rigor to the _Magna Carta_.

That said, AI development is incredibly capital-intensive, and startups are pressured to follow VCs’ classic playbook: “drive the price low to gain market share, then start squeezing for profits once people can’t leave you.” Thus, we believe the most effective option is not for VCs or financial companies to fund AI efforts, but for _countries_ to invest in developing sovereign AI. This does not mean every country needs a frontier-level model; rather, each country should find a niche across cost, use cases, policy, politics, and other factors so that building its own AI model is both beneficial and sustainable. This also has the added benefit of kickstarting an _ecosystem_ within a country for AI development, which in the long run means better talent retention, a more mature tech funding landscape and further technological development.

## Sovereign Ecosystems

We find ourselves in a good position to prove this idea possible. We believe Taiwan has the potential to play a meaningful role in the global AI conversation. Yet we have largely watched from the sidelines as opportunities to develop an ecosystem passed time and time again. This is not for a lack of trying. Taiwan gave building our AI ecosystems a shot, but limited funding and poor execution made it difficult to sustain.

As a student-led organization, we lack much of the bureaucracy of established institutions and have the freedom to try something that would otherwise be a moonshot. There is a reason so many startups begin with founders who have just graduated, or have yet to do so. It’s quite useful to have relatively little to preserve and a great deal to prove. If we can help jumpstart that ecosystem here, we believe we can demonstrate that a country which has not led in the AI race can still gain a say in how this technology develops.

Furthermore, ecosystems start with adoption, and in Taiwan’s case, we believe that for wide adoption of language models to happen, cheap and local inference are both non-negotiable. Cheap is easy to achieve through smaller models; having models that are both cheap and smart, though, is not.

We thus aim for the middle ground. Instead of blowing up cost by training a large generalist model, or betting entirely on our ability to be much more compute-efficient than all frontier labs combined, we choose an intermediate model: a small but generally smart base that is easy to fine-tune into any use case with relatively little data. This further gives people a reason to participate, thereby facilitating know-how and collaborations in both industry and academia to take the low-hanging fruit we grew.

Planning backwards from the goal of adoption, we start by training a 1-billion-parameter model that is exceptionally easy to tune, with the ambition of matching models several times its size. We believe cheap enough deployment and accessible customization give us a chance to convince more companies that developing models is something they can take part in themselves. If we can show that this is both possible and useful, we hope more organizations will be willing to commit talent and funding. Our first model is meant to be a starting point for that ecosystem.

## Relentless openness

Acquiring funding is only the first step, the second is figuring out how to put the research this money generates to good use. Throughout centuries, we’ve relied on academia to publish and drive public knowledge. Yet in a capital-intensive field such as AI, academic labs have nowhere near the compute or funding available to frontier labs. This, however, does not mean we must accept that academic research will always lag. If we pool our talent, ideas, and resources, we might have a shot at catching up.

Too often, labs work behind closed doors and spend their limited resources rediscovering things other researchers have already learned. A finished model or paper gives us some of the result, but much less of the experience required to arrive there. Which ideas failed? What went wrong during training? Why was one approach chosen over another? This knowledge is expensive to acquire, yet we are often left in a state where each lab must reinvent the wheel.

We greatly admire Marin’s concept of an _open lab_, and wish to adopt a similar approach at Cerulean. Those of us fortunate enough to receive the compute needed for our work should leave behind enough of a record that others do not need to start from scratch. A lab should not have to repeat an entire training run, including its failed experiments, to understand the decisions that shaped it. Researchers should be able to retrace our work and build on our experience, even when they cannot afford to reproduce everything themselves.

Furthermore, governments, like investors, have their own reasons to push for certain things to be built and for others to stay private. This is why we believe sovereign AI needs relentless openness. When we work in public, it’s harder for any one funder to change what we build or release without people seeing it. And once the work is out in the world, others can keep building on it even if the lab is pushed to change course.

We believe our kind of openness serves two purposes. It subjects our work to public scrutiny, allowing others to question whether our decisions serve the people we claim to work for. It also gives us a better chance of closing the gap with frontier labs. The open world has an enormous diversity of talent, ideas, and resources. Much of that potential is lost when every group must independently build the same foundations before it can contribute anything new.

We cannot expect to match the resources of the largest labs on our own. We can, however, use what we collectively have much better.

## Our bets

At Cerulean, we are betting on three things.

First, that a sufficiently funded, public-sector-backed lab, working with relentless openness, can reach the frontier in a particular niche. A country does not need to compete across every dimension of AI development to make a meaningful contribution. It needs to choose an area where its own needs and capabilities give it a reason to commit, and support the work long enough for it to succeed.

Second, more open efforts can help academic research build on itself. If we share the experience behind our results, each new lab can begin a little further along. We believe this accumulated knowledge can help close the gap between academic and frontier research, and allow a much wider group of people to contribute to the next major advances in AI.

Third, making sovereign AI practical gives more countries a reason to participate. The more countries, institutions and communities capable of developing this technology, the harder it becomes for its future to be determined by only a few companies or nations. This requires solving enough of the practical problems that participation becomes worth funding and sustaining.

We believe Taiwan is in a particularly good position to begin this work, and we hope Cerulean can help move our country towards that possibility.

## Open skies

Cerulean is named after the sky. We romantically admire its openness, its vastness, and the sense of possibility it gives us. We see our models as birds: small, intelligent and ever so lovely. We see ourselves as a lab without walls, with the capacity to do meaningful work and a deeply human reason for doing it.

We want a world where everyone has access to this technology, and everyone has a say in how it shapes their lives and the society we share. We are starting Cerulean because we believe that world is possible, and want to help build it.

When that day comes, _sky’s the limit_.
