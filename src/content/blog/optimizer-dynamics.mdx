Optimizer dynamics can distinguish optimizers, but mostly can't predict the runs within the same optimizer family, with one candidate exception.

All training and logging code is on <a href={REPO_URL}>GitHub</a>.

## Background

In transformer model training, is it possible to retrieve information beyond the loss curve that sheds light on hyperparameter tuning and optimizer choice?

To answer this question, we train a Llama 3.2-style model<Sidenote title="The Llama 3 Herd of Models" authors="Llama Team, Meta AI" venue="arXiv 2024" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783" /> with 174M parameters and a Llama 3.2 1B model. We call the 174M model the "proxy model" and the 1B model the "main model". Models of different scales allow us to verify whether the findings on the proxy model transfer to the larger model. Statistical metrics on the gradient and update step are logged throughout training. We call them optimizer dynamics, which include vector length, angle difference, relative scale, etc.

Throughout this article, we specifically investigate the following questions of interest:

- Can optimizer dynamics predict the performance of optimizers?
- Can we manipulate the metrics to affect loss?
- Are there differences in optimizer dynamics between block types? Can we choose the most "suitable" optimizer for each block?

### Metric: Rank

The rank of a matrix is the maximum number of linearly independent rows or columns it contains. While rank measures the output dimension of the weight, it is sensitive to noise. Stable rank, on the other hand, is robust to noise. Stable rank is defined as the ratio of the sum of all squared singular values to the square of the maximum singular value. It roughly measures "how many directions are actually useful".

We calculate the stable rank of each weight matrix, including the embedding, MLP, and attention matrices.

### Overshoot

We want to investigate the overshoot behavior: does the optimizer step go past the minimum along its own direction?

$$
\text{overshoot} = \frac{\cos(\Delta W_{t-1},\, g_t)}{\cos(\Delta W_{t-1},\, -g_{t-1})}
$$

This compares the gradient along the step direction at the start and at the end of the step. If overshoot is positive, it typically means the step goes past the minimum along its own direction. On the other hand, the step stays on the slope toward the minimum when overshoot is negative.

### Common metrics may include biases

**The rise in sharpness during the decay is a measurement artifact.**

<figure>
    <img
        src="/images/blog/optimizer-dynamics/fig07_sharpness_bias.png"
        alt="Left: directional sharpness during training, rising during decay; right: directional sharpness multiplied by the learning rate, with the rise removed"
        loading="lazy"
        decoding="async"
    />
    <figcaption>
        Left: directional sharpness during training. The bias grows as 1/LR
        during the learning rate decay. Right: directional sharpness multiplied
        by the learning rate, removing the 1/LR scale.
    </figcaption>
</figure>

The sharpness is defined as follows<Sidenote title="MiniCPM: Unveiling the Potential of Small Language Models with Scalable Training Strategies" authors="Hu et al." venue="2024" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06395" />, as an approximate estimate of the second-order directional derivative (calculated for each batch):

$$
D_2 = \frac{(g_t - g_{t-1}) \cdot v_{t-1}}{\|v_{t-1}\|^2} \simeq \frac{v^\top H v}{\|v\|^2}, \quad \text{where } v = \Delta W,\ H = \text{Hessian}
$$

This measures the sharpness of the loss along the step direction. However, the estimate carries a bias from batch noise, which pushes it upward during the learning rate decay by up to about $1/\eta$. That is the reason for the rapid rise in the sharpness metric, **not that the surface gets sharper.**

Therefore, we multiply D2 by the learning rate to remove the 1/LR trend.

<details>
<summary>Do the math</summary>

Definition of D2:

$$
D_2 = \frac{(g_t - g_{t-1}) \cdot v_{t-1}}{\|v_{t-1}\|^2}
$$

Given that the batch gradient contains batch noise, we have:

$$
\begin{gathered}
g = G + n, \quad n \sim \text{i.i.d.} \\
\text{where } G \text{ is the true gradient, } n \text{ is the batch noise}
\end{gathered}
$$

Assume an SGD update for simplicity:

$$
\text{SGD:} \quad v_{t-1} = -\eta\, g_{t-1} = -\eta\,(G_{t-1} + n_{t-1})
$$

Expand D2 as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_2 &= \frac{(G_t + n_t - G_{t-1} - n_{t-1})\, v_{t-1}}{\|v_{t-1}\|^2} \\
&= \frac{(G_t - G_{t-1})\, v_{t-1}}{\|v_{t-1}\|^2} + \frac{n_t\, v_{t-1}}{\|v_{t-1}\|^2} - \frac{n_{t-1}\, v_{t-1}}{\|v_{t-1}\|^2} \\[6pt]
&\simeq \underbrace{\frac{v_{t-1}^\top H\, v_{t-1}}{\|v_{t-1}\|^2}}_{\text{true curvature}} + \frac{n_t\, v_{t-1}}{\|v_{t-1}\|^2} - \frac{n_{t-1}\, v_{t-1}}{\|v_{t-1}\|^2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that D2 contains not only curvature but also biases. Next, we explore the expected value of the bias.

The first bias term. Noise at time t is independent of the past, so the expected value of noise is 0.

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{n_t\, v_{t-1}}{\|v_{t-1}\|^2}\right] = \mathbb{E}\left[n_t\right]\, \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{v_{t-1}}{\|v_{t-1}\|^2}\right] = 0
$$

The second bias term. Noise at time t-1 is not independent of the previous step. Expanding the term with the SGD update, we can see that the step also contains that noise.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{n_{t-1}\, v_{t-1}}{\|v_{t-1}\|^2}\right] &= \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{-\eta\, n_{t-1}\,(G_{t-1}+n_{t-1})}{\eta^2\,\|G_{t-1}+n_{t-1}\|^2}\right] \\
&= \frac{1}{-\eta}\, \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{n_{t-1}\,G_{t-1}+n_{t-1}^2}{\|G_{t-1}+n_{t-1}\|^2}\right] \\
&= \frac{1}{-\eta}\, \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{n_{t-1}^2}{\|G_{t-1}+n_{t-1}\|^2}\right]
\end{aligned}
$$

using that noise and the true gradient are independent, so $\mathbb{E}\left[n_{t-1}\, G_{t-1}\right] = \mathbb{E}\left[n_{t-1}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[G_{t-1}\right] = 0$.

The second bias term biases the curvature estimate upward during the learning rate decay by up to about $1/\eta$:

$$
D_2 \simeq \underbrace{\frac{v_{t-1}^\top H\, v_{t-1}}{\|v_{t-1}\|^2}}_{\text{true curvature}} + \frac{1}{\eta}\, \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{n_{t-1}^2}{\|G_{t-1}+n_{t-1}\|^2}\right]
$$

</details>

We further measure the noise by using a probe batch to calculate sharpness. A probe batch is a holdout batch that is not used in training. Therefore, probe sharpness is free of this bias.

There is indeed a gap between the batch estimate and the probe sharpness, and it stays constant throughout the stable phase. The right figure shows that the bias doesn't simply scale as 1/LR. The noise itself is different across learning rates.

## Metrics under different learning rates

For each optimizer, we train proxy models with different learning rates. The learning rate tuned by the sweep is treated as the baseline. At least 2 learning rates are lower than the baseline, and at least 2 are higher. None of the proxy runs diverged. For Muon and NorMuon, we keep the LR of the AdamW parameter groups fixed.

We first explore possible indicators for the best learning rate band on the proxy model—specifically, metrics whose values follow a U-shape or an inverted U-shape across LRs, with the best learning rate band sitting at the top or bottom. We then run a learning rate sweep on the main model to test whether the indicators we found on the proxy model transfer.

In short, most best-LR indicators found on the proxy are screening noise or simply track the LR value. The one that survives is probe sharpness for Muon: it peaks at about LR 0.01 on both models, around the best LR band.

First, the loss itself across each sweep: every optimizer shows a clear best-LR band, with the final loss climbing on either side of it.

#### Is the sharpness peak in Muon and NorMuon real?

This is a natural concern, since the usual sharpness estimator is inflated by batch noise. Measuring the directional sharpness on a fixed probe batch removes this noise and lets us test whether the sharpness indicator is pure noise or carries information.

With the probe batch, the sharpness still peaks at the same learning rate. The peak sits on the best learning rate for Muon and one step off for NorMuon.

    <figcaption>Probe directional sharpness on Muon.</figcaption>

Why does the sharpness peak appear? Global probe sharpness equals each block's sharpness weighted by that block's share of the update. In the Muon and NorMuon sweeps, the LR of the AdamW blocks is fixed. The embedding (AdamW at a fixed LR) dominates the update at low Muon LR. The global peak is the point where the matrix updates take over. The peak may simply reflect the balance between the embedding LR and the Muon LR. Alternatively, this balance may be exactly why the best LR performs best. Our current experiments can't tell these two explanations apart.

The figure below breaks the global sharpness down into the contribution of each block type.

In the figure, we can see that nearly all of the peak comes from the attention and MLP contributions, which rise and then fall. The embedding itself contributes little. The peak lands where the embedding's share drops from about 90% toward 40%.

## Can we manipulate the metrics to affect loss?

Based on the above analysis, there are indeed different metric patterns between optimizers.

We then ask: if we somehow manipulate these metrics, will the final loss be affected?

We take NorMuon as the baseline since it is our best setting. We create several variants to manipulate the metrics. These variants are trained on the proxy model with 1B tokens.

<aside class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">ℹ️ Seed noise</p>

Throughout our experiment on the proxy model, the seed-to-seed difference in final loss is about 0.001–0.004. We consider loss changes under 0.004 to be noise.

The seed-to-seed noise on the main model is up to 0.016.

</aside>

In the overshoot metrics, V and K in attention overshoot the most. Muon orthogonalizes each weight matrix as a whole. But q_proj, k_proj, and v_proj are really stacks of independent attention heads: 12 query heads and 4 shared key/value heads. Therefore, we orthogonalize Q, K, and V per attention head instead of as whole matrices. This method is called MuonSplit.<Sidenote title="When and Why Grouping Attention Heads Accelerates Muon Optimization" authors="Zhang et al." venue="2026" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08933v1" />

Splitting the heads lowers the overshoot, meaning that the step becomes more precise and the model lands closer to the minimum along its direction.

## Key findings

- Optimizer families show distinct patterns in rank, oscillation, alignment and overshoot, and these rankings are the same at both the 174M and 1B scales.
- Directly manipulating the metrics doesn't change the final loss within optimizer families.
- Both interventions had the same (null) effect on loss at both scales, and their effect on rank and alignment also carried over.
- All optimizers overshoot their steps through the minimum along the step direction in 59 of 59 measurements.
- Most best-LR-band indicators don't transfer from the proxy model to the main model. Probe sharpness does transfer for Muon. However, since the best LR stays at 0.01 on both models, we can't yet tell whether the peak tracks the best LR or simply stays at 0.01.
- Common estimators can be biased by measurement bias. In our case, the sharp rise in sharpness along the update direction during the LR decay phase comes from an estimator bias that scales as 1/LR.

## Limitations

- The learning rate sweep experiment uses one seed per configuration. Small differences in metrics may come from seed noise. More seeds are needed to verify whether the relationship between LR and dynamics metrics exists.
- Probe-batch measurements were only run for Muon and NorMuon in the learning rate sweep.
- The data-to-model size ratio differs between the proxy model (174M model trained on 1B or 6B tokens) and main model (1B model trained on 1B or 3B tokens). This is unlike common practice, where model and data scale together.
- AdamW is weakly tuned: only its LR was swept, and other hyperparameters were tuned on the proxy and reused.
